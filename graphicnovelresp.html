Graphic Novel: Motherhood in crisis

Motherhood in Crisis is an interactive graphic novel by Al Jazeera that follows four women, Memunatu, Heela, Kadiatu, and Tetteh, as they navigate pregnancy and survival in Sierra Leone, a country where one in ten women die during childbirth. The piece combines illustration, interactivity, and real documentary video footage to pull readers in before confronting them with difficult realities. Presenting these stories through a comic format initially feels disarming, almost like entertainment, but the gap between the gentle form and the heavy content becomes one of its strongest qualities. Requiring the reader to click through each woman's story creates a sense of involvement that keeps each narrative feeling personal rather than letting the pain blur together.

The roots of these women's suffering run deep in broken systems. Sierra Leone's healthcare, which is already weak, was made far worse by the Ebola epidemic and then COVID-19, causing many citizens to distrust hospitals, especially pregnant women who depend on reliable care. When transportation is too expensive, the village is far away, and people seem to vanish after seeking treatment, it makes sense that many would avoid formal medicine altogether. This distrust creates space for harmful figures, like the spiritual healer in Kadiatu's story who blamed her for the deaths of her own children. The same pattern shows up in the U.S., where "free birth" coaches take advantage of people's fear of the medical system, selling programs while ignoring the deaths their advice has caused.

Of the four stories, Tetteh's catches my attention. She was imprisoned for defending herself against an abusive husband, then gave birth and briefly raised her son Alie inside a women's prison, which is something that feels almost unimaginable from an American perspective. Her story, like the others, points to a larger pattern of structural injustice: cultural norms that limit women's choices, financial barriers that block access to healthcare, and legal systems that punish women for surviving. It leaves the reader hoping that groups like Advocaid and Prison Watch, which were supporting women like her, have been able to keep going.

The piece ends on a rather uncertain note, with updates from 2020 sharing difficult news about the closure of centers like the Aberdeen Women's Centre and the Rainbo Centre in Bo due to COVID-19. It is a reminder that even the small support systems available to these women can fall apart quickly. The pandemic, like Ebola before it, did not create these problems but made existing ones much worse. Six years later, one can only hope these organizations have recovered and are still reaching the people who need them most. More than anything, the graphic novel asks its reader to pay attention and to keep caring long after the last page.
