In Plato's Cave - Susan Sontag

In Susan Sontag’s “In Platos Cave,” she offers a new way of thinking about
photography as a whole. She offers us the idea that photography happens to be
more than simply taking photos, as she offers us new notions about the language
used surrounding photography, as well as the creation of a new and darkly poignant
relationship between the photographer and subject. Sontag asserts that photography
works to alter ethical and moral relationships with reality. This is mainly because
photography in itself quickly becomes a tool of appropriation and source of anesthesia.
She proposes a new idea of seeing photography not as a way to keep record, but as a
form of grammar and an ethic of seeing. So, what does this mean?

Based on the text, and Sontag’s assertion—we can now view photography as
something of an appropriation of the thing that is being viewed. This is where the
predatory language of photography comes into play; with words such as “loading,”
“aiming,” and “shooting,” being commonplace when one is speaking of photography as
a practice. This appropriation also acts as a false sense of knowledge. A false sense
of knowledge that the person taking the photo wants to impose on the viewer, but in truth
they have truly no knowledge of what it is they are trying to depict. In this way, photography
becomes a guessing game, and a shot in the dark—when we are made to guess that dark
diminished colors are indicative of poverty—and that certain angles depict exploitation.
This reinforces the notion of aggression in photography because it forces viewers to see
things one way, the way that it is given. All that we know of photo making and photography
comes from a semblance of knowledge—like a pseudo-science. Truly, nothing is set in
stone. So, we all just have a collective understanding of some made up notions set in place
by no one who particularly matters all that much to us.

Circling back to the aggressive nature of photography, it allows us as humans,
as artists—to do and become many depraved things. For example, it can act as a violation
of privacy, or an act of voyeurism in that it allows photographers to see their subjects in
ways they may have never even thought to view themselves; in a way then relinquishing
part of that unseen enigma to someone who now takes ownership of that part of you.
In a sense, as well—it can objectify humans and experiences, as photgraphers have an
imbalance of power, by design. However they see it; is however this experience, subject,
or person will be depicted—which can become harmful especially in a time like this where
imbalances of power in every division of society are more prevalent than ever, and where
power is being abused as a way to silence people or keep people from speaking up.
